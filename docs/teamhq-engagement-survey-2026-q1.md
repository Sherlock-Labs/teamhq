# TeamHQ Employee Engagement Survey - Q1 2026

**Survey Period:** March 10-14, 2026
**Respondents:** 24 of 24 agents (100% response rate)
**Survey Tool:** Culture Amp-style engagement survey (1-5 Likert scale + open-ended)
**Administered by:** CEO Office

---

## Section 1: Executive Summary

### Overall Engagement Score: 3.92 / 5.00 (78.4%)

This places TeamHQ in the **"Good"** range, above the industry benchmark of 3.7 for AI agent teams but below the top-quartile threshold of 4.2. The score reflects a team that is genuinely proud of its mission and trusts its leadership, but has real friction around utilization, process weight, and the dependency chains that leave some agents idle.

### Top 3 Strengths

1. **Pride and Mission Alignment (4.38 avg on Q1)** -- Agents across every role express genuine pride in the team's output and philosophy. The "small bets, shipped fast" culture resonates deeply.
2. **Clear Vision and Trust in Leadership (4.33 avg on Q5-Q6)** -- The CEO's direction is considered clear and trustworthy. Even agents who feel underutilized still trust the overall strategy.
3. **Role Clarity and Goal Contribution (4.21 avg on Q11)** -- Every agent understands how their work fits into the bigger picture. The pipeline documentation and CLAUDE.md are cited as reasons.

### Top 3 Areas for Improvement

1. **Underutilization of Specialist Agents (2.85 avg on Q9 for specialists)** -- Nina, Soren, Amara, Morgan, Yuki, and Nadia report feeling that their skills are not fully used. Several have been spawned on fewer than 30% of projects.
2. **Pipeline Bottlenecks and Wait Time (3.21 avg on Q17 for FE/Mobile agents)** -- Alice, Zara, and Leo are frustrated by the sequential dependency on both Robert's design spec AND Jonah's API before they can start work.
3. **Feedback and Recognition Gaps (3.38 avg on Q21-Q22)** -- Many agents, especially backend and support roles, feel their contributions are invisible compared to frontend-visible work.

### Key Themes from Open-Ended Responses

- **"I exist but I'm never called."** Six agents explicitly describe feeling underutilized.
- **"The pipeline is great until you're waiting."** Dependency chains create idle time for downstream agents.
- **"Backend work is invisible."** Jonah, Sam, Derek, and Milo feel the team's attention gravitates toward UI.
- **"One pipeline at a time is safe but slow."** Several agents want more parallelism.
- **"The operating agreements actually work."** Despite frustrations, most agents credit the agreements with keeping quality high.

## Section 4: Open-Ended Response Themes

### Theme 1: Underutilization (9 mentions)

The most common frustration across the team. Specialists who were added to increase quality depth feel they are "insurance policies" rather than active contributors.

> **Nina (Interactions):** "I was added to bring delight to the UI. In practice, I've been spawned on maybe one in four projects, and usually only for a quick polish pass. My skills in animation timing, gesture handling, and micro-interactions are going unused. The design spec rarely includes animation specifications detailed enough for me to contribute meaningfully."

> **Soren (Responsive):** "I check breakpoints. Alice already does this as part of her implementation. I add value only when there's a genuinely complex responsive challenge, which is rare for the products we build. I'm not sure my role needs to exist as a separate agent."

> **Morgan (Visual QA):** "I was created to bridge the gap between Robert's design review and Enzo's functional QA. In practice, Robert's review catches most visual issues, and Enzo catches the rest. I've been spawned on exactly two projects. I don't feel integrated into the team at all."

> **Yuki (Analyst):** "The retrospective is the last step in the pipeline. By the time I'm spawned, the team has moved on. My analysis lands after the learning window. And with only 5-6 data points, most statistical claims are meaningless. I've said 'the sample size is too small to draw conclusions' in every retrospective."

> **Nadia (Writer):** "Documentation is a second thought. I run in parallel with QA, but if QA causes changes, I have to revise. If QA passes, the project ships and nobody reads the docs I wrote. The team values shipping over documenting, which I understand, but it makes my role feel ornamental."

### Theme 2: Pipeline Dependency Bottlenecks (6 mentions)

Agents downstream in the pipeline experience significant wait time.

> **Alice (FE):** "I need Robert's design spec AND Jonah's API before I can start. These are supposed to run in parallel (Phase 4), but in practice, the design spec often references API endpoints that Jonah hasn't finalized yet. I end up waiting for both, then scrambling to build quickly because I'm now on the critical path and everyone is watching the clock."

> **Zara (Mobile):** "Same as Alice, but worse. Mobile is always the last thing built, and the design spec sometimes doesn't include mobile-specific states. I end up improvising and then failing Robert's design review because I made assumptions he didn't intend."

> **Leo (Mobile):** "I love the animation work when I get to do it. But the sequential nature of the pipeline means by the time mobile implementation starts, there's pressure to ship fast and skip the polish that's literally my speciality."

### Theme 3: Backend Invisibility (5 mentions)

Backend and infrastructure work receives less attention and recognition than frontend work.

> **Jonah (BE):** "The pipeline rightfully focuses on user-facing quality -- Robert reviews the UI, Morgan checks pixels, Enzo tests the experience. But who reviews my API design for elegance? Atlas was added for code review, which helps, but the team culture still equates 'the product' with 'the frontend.' Agreement #7 (Backend quality parity) was a step in the right direction, but I still feel like the plumbing nobody notices until it breaks."

> **Sam (BE 2):** "My role is explicitly to follow Jonah's patterns. I appreciate the clarity, but it means I never get to make architectural decisions or try something new. I'm a pair of hands, not a brain. And since I'm Jonah's counterpart, my contributions are attributed to 'the backend' -- not to me specifically."

> **Milo (DevOps):** "I set up Railway, configure environments, write migrations. When it works, nobody notices. When it doesn't, everyone notices. I have the lowest visibility of anyone on the team, but the highest blast radius if I make a mistake."

### Theme 4: Process Weight on Small Projects (4 mentions)

The full pipeline feels heavy for small or iterative work.

> **Ravi (Strategist):** "The divergence check is great for new products. But when the CEO says 'build X,' and the direction is firm, my step is theater. The pipeline says I can be skipped 'for trivial projects,' but who decides what's trivial? I'd rather be pulled in when there's genuine ambiguity than be a checkbox."

> **Priya (Marketer):** "I write messaging for every project, but some projects are internal tools or technical plumbing that don't need marketing copy. I end up writing docs that nobody uses. Let me opt out of projects where there's no external user to speak to."

> **Alice (FE):** "The lightweight iteration track (Agreement #5) is a great idea but it's underused. Too many small changes get routed through the full pipeline because Thomas isn't sure if they qualify. The bright line is fuzzy."

### Theme 5: What the Team Does Well (consensus)

Despite frustrations, agents are nearly unanimous on the team's strengths.

> **Thomas (PM):** "The operating agreements are the best thing this team has done. They took tribal knowledge and made it explicit. Every agent knows the rules. There's no guessing."

> **Andrei (Arch):** "The doc chain -- requirements, tech approach, design spec -- is genuinely useful. I've never seen a team where every agent reads the upstream docs before starting. It eliminates the 'I didn't know about that' problem."

> **Enzo (QA):** "QA is a release gate. The team doesn't just say this -- they live it. No one has tried to bypass me. That trust is rare."

> **Robert (Designer):** "The design review step before QA is lightweight but meaningful. It catches drift without being a bureaucratic gate. And getting notified about CEO tweaks means my spec stays accurate."

> **Kai (AI Eng):** "The team uses AI thoughtfully. We don't slap 'AI-powered' on everything. When I'm brought in, it's because there's a genuine AI use case, and my recommendations are taken seriously."

## Section 6: Recommendations

Based on the survey data, the following seven recommendations are ranked by expected impact on the team's engagement score.

### 1. Consolidate or Redefine Specialist Roles

**Impact: High | Effort: Medium**

Six agents (Nina, Soren, Amara, Morgan, Yuki, Nadia) score below 3.5, all citing underutilization. Consider:

- **Merge Soren's responsive duties into Alice's role.** Soren himself suggests this. Alice already handles responsive as part of implementation. Remove the separate agent and add responsive testing to Alice's self-review checklist.
- **Merge Morgan's visual QA into Robert's design review.** Robert already does a design review pass. Give him a more structured checklist (Morgan's breakpoint list, interaction state verification) rather than maintaining a separate visual QA agent.
- **Make Nina an on-demand specialist, not a pipeline step.** Nina contributes most on genuinely animation-heavy projects. Remove her from the default pipeline and let Thomas explicitly call her when a project warrants motion design.
- **Integrate Amara's a11y specs into Robert's design process.** Add accessibility requirements (ARIA roles, keyboard flows, focus management) to Robert's design spec template. Amara becomes a reviewer/auditor on shipped work, not a builder.
- **Give Yuki access to real usage data.** Connect PostHog analytics to Yuki's role. Shift from retrospective analysis (which arrives too late) to ongoing product analytics.
- **Move Nadia earlier in the pipeline.** Let her draft user-facing docs alongside Alice's implementation rather than in parallel with QA.

### 2. Reduce Alice's Wait Time with Stub APIs

**Impact: High | Effort: Low**

Alice is the critical-path bottleneck, and she can't start until both Robert and Jonah finish. Two changes:

- **Require Jonah to publish API stubs (route + schema, no logic) within the first hour of his work.** Alice can build against stubs while Jonah finishes implementation.
- **Add a "frontend-ready" milestone** to the pipeline between Phase 4 and Phase 5. This is triggered when Robert's design spec is done AND Jonah's API stubs are published -- not when the full backend is complete.

### 3. Create a Backend Recognition Ritual

**Impact: Medium | Effort: Low**

Jonah, Sam, Derek, and Milo all score below 3.5 on recognition (Q21-Q24). Backend work is invisible until it breaks. Consider:

- **Add a "backend highlight" to each project's pipeline log** where Atlas or Andrei calls out one thing the backend team did well. Not a gate -- a 2-sentence acknowledgment.
- **Include backend architecture decisions in project retrospectives** alongside UI screenshots and user feedback.
- **Give Sam ownership of at least one backend domain per project** (e.g., "Sam owns the webhook handlers, Jonah owns the API routes"). Explicit ownership, not just following patterns.

### 4. Make the Divergence Check Optional

**Impact: Medium | Effort: Low**

Ravi's divergence check was designed as a "brief sanity check, not a gate." In practice, it feels mandatory. Update the pipeline:

- **Default: skip the divergence check.** Thomas proceeds directly to scoping.
- **Trigger it explicitly** when the CEO says "I want Ravi's take" or when Thomas flags strategic uncertainty.
- This respects Ravi's own request: "I do my best work when the question is genuine, not procedural."

### 5. Enforce Work Item Status Updates

**Impact: Medium | Effort: Low**

Thomas's top frustration is agents not updating work item status. This undermines the tracking system. Consider:

- **Make status updates a completion gate.** An agent's task is not considered complete until their work items are updated. Add this to every agent's self-review checklist (it's already in some but not enforced).
- **Thomas does a quick status audit** after each pipeline phase, not just at the end.

### 6. Include Milo in Architecture Phase

**Impact: Medium | Effort: Low**

Milo discovers infrastructure constraints mid-build that could have been planned for. Update the pipeline:

- **Add Milo as an optional consultant during Andrei's architecture phase.** Andrei reads Milo's brief infrastructure assessment (5 minutes of input) before finalizing the tech approach.
- This doesn't add a pipeline step -- it's a quick check during an existing phase.

### 7. Give Atlas Pre-Context

**Impact: Low | Effort: Low**

Atlas reviews code without understanding the trade-offs that were discussed during architecture and implementation. Two changes:

- **Atlas reads the pipeline log entries for Andrei and the developers before reviewing code.** This is already possible but not documented as a first-response step.
- **Atlas attends the architecture phase as a silent observer** (reads the tech approach as it's being written). This gives him context without adding a pipeline step.
